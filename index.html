Getting started

How to use this handbook

Work through the chapters in order. For every product or service, keep records of purchase cost, direct labour, packaging, delivery, overhead, selling price, tax and payment dates. Use actual business data rather than estimates whenever possible.

Important: This is an educational business guide, not legal, tax, accounting or investment advice. Tax rates, filing rules and accounting treatments change. For Indian GST, income tax, TDS and statutory compliance, verify current rules with the official tax portal or a qualified accountant.

The five numbers every entrepreneur should know

Revenue
How much you earn from sales before expenses.
Gross profit
Revenue less cost of goods sold (COGS).
Net profit
What remains after all business expenses.
Cash balance
Money actually available to pay obligations.
Break-even sales
Sales needed to cover fixed and variable costs.
Working capital
Short-term resources available for operations.
Foundation

1. Number skills for business

Core operations

  • Addition: combine sales, expenses or quantities.
  • Subtraction: find balances, differences or remaining stock.
  • Multiplication: unit price × quantity.
  • Division: total cost ÷ units, or total ÷ number of periods.
  • Order of operations: brackets, powers, division/multiplication, addition/subtraction (BODMAS).

Fractions and decimals

A fraction represents part of a whole. Convert a fraction to a percentage by multiplying by 100. Convert a percentage to a decimal by dividing by 100.

3/4 = 0.75 = 75%   |   12.5% = 0.125

Units and conversions

Check whether a price is per piece, kilogram, litre, metre, dozen, box or month. Convert all figures to a common unit before comparing suppliers or calculating margins.

Example: A 25 kg bag costs ₹1,000. Cost per kg = ₹1,000 ÷ 25 = ₹40/kg.
Everyday calculations

2. Percentages, ratios and averages

Percentage = (Part ÷ Whole) × 100
Percentage change = ((New − Old) ÷ Old) × 100
Ratio a:b means a ÷ b; simplify by dividing both terms by the same number.
Weighted average = Σ(value × weight) ÷ Σ(weights)
Example — sales growth: Monthly sales rise from ₹80,000 to ₹1,00,000. Increase = ₹20,000. Growth = 20,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 25%.
Example — ingredient mix: A recipe uses 2 parts concentrate and 5 parts water. Total = 7 parts. For 14 litres, concentrate = 14 × 2/7 = 4 litres.

Business uses: growth rates, expense ratios, market share, salary increases, product mixes, conversion rates and supplier comparisons.

Product economics

3. Costing and pricing

Separate costs into direct and indirect costs, and fixed and variable costs.

Cost typeMeaningExamples
DirectCan be traced to a product or serviceMaterials, product-specific labour, packaging
Indirect / overheadSupports the business generallyRent, office internet, administration
FixedDoes not change with output in the short runMonthly rent, base salary
VariableChanges with units sold or producedMaterials, per-order shipping, sales fees
Total cost = Fixed costs + Variable costs
Unit cost = Total production cost ÷ Units produced
Landed cost = Purchase price + freight + insurance + duties + other acquisition costs

Cost-plus pricing

Selling price before tax = Unit cost × (1 + Markup %)
Example: Unit cost ₹400 and markup 25%. Price = ₹400 × 1.25 = ₹500 before applicable tax.

Price required for a target gross margin

Selling price = Unit cost ÷ (1 − Target gross margin)
If unit cost is ₹400 and target gross margin is 20%, price = 400 ÷ 0.80 = ₹500.
Markup is not margin. A 25% markup on ₹400 gives ₹500. Gross margin is ₹100 ÷ ₹500 = 20%.
Profitability

4. Profit, loss and margins

Gross profit = Net sales − Cost of goods sold (COGS)
Gross margin % = (Gross profit ÷ Net sales) × 100
Operating profit = Gross profit − Operating expenses
Net profit = Total income − Total expenses
Net profit margin % = (Net profit ÷ Net sales) × 100
Example: Net sales ₹2,00,000; COGS ₹1,20,000; operating expenses ₹50,000; interest and other expenses ₹5,000. Gross profit = ₹80,000; operating profit = ₹30,000; net profit = ₹25,000. Net margin = 12.5%.

Track profit by product, customer, channel and month. A high-sales product may still be unprofitable after returns, discounts, delivery and platform fees.

Selling

5. Discounts, commission and trade terms

Discount amount = Marked price × Discount rate
Net selling price = Marked price − Discount amount
Commission = Eligible sales × Commission rate
Effective discount for successive discounts = 1 − (1 − d₁)(1 − d₂)
Example: List price ₹2,000, then 10% and 5% discounts. Final price = 2,000 × 0.90 × 0.95 = ₹1,710. Total effective discount = 14.5%, not 15%.

Before approving a discount, calculate the contribution left after variable costs. A discount can increase sales while reducing total profit.

India-focused tax arithmetic

6. GST and tax calculations

GST is an indirect tax. In common intra-state transactions, CGST and SGST apply; in inter-state transactions, IGST generally applies. The applicable rate and treatment depend on the goods or services, place and nature of supply, registration and current law.

GST amount = Taxable value × GST rate ÷ 100
Invoice total = Taxable value + GST amount
Taxable value from a tax-inclusive amount = Inclusive amount ÷ (1 + GST rate/100)
Example — tax added: Taxable value ₹1,000 at an illustrative 18% rate. GST = ₹180; invoice total = ₹1,180.
Example — tax included: Price including 18% GST is ₹1,180. Taxable value = 1,180 ÷ 1.18 = ₹1,000; GST component = ₹180.

Reconciliation concepts

  • Output tax: tax collected/owed on taxable sales.
  • Input tax credit (ITC): eligible tax credit on business purchases, subject to statutory conditions.
  • Net GST payable: output tax less eligible ITC, adjusted as required by law.
  • Keep valid invoices, credit notes, purchase records and return reconciliations.
Do not assume every purchase qualifies for ITC or every sale uses the same rate. Verify current rules and classification on the official GST portal or with a tax professional.
Finance

7. Interest, savings and loans

Simple interest = Principal × Rate × Time ÷ 100
Simple-interest maturity amount = Principal + Interest
Compound amount = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

For compound interest, P is principal, r is annual rate as a decimal, n is compounding periods per year and t is years.

Simple interest example: ₹50,000 at 12% per year for 2 years: interest = ₹50,000 × 12 × 2 ÷ 100 = ₹12,000.

Loan and EMI concepts

EMI = P × i × (1+i)^N ÷ ((1+i)^N − 1)

P = loan principal; i = monthly interest rate as a decimal; N = number of monthly payments. The EMI includes interest and principal. Compare the total repayment, processing fees, insurance, prepayment charges and rate-reset terms—not just the EMI.

Effective annual rate

Effective annual rate = (1 + nominal rate/n)^n − 1
Survival and liquidity

8. Cash flow and working capital

Closing cash = Opening cash + Cash inflows − Cash outflows
Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities
Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Quick ratio = (Cash + Short-term investments + Receivables) ÷ Current liabilities
Example: Opening cash ₹60,000; customer receipts ₹1,40,000; payments ₹1,55,000. Closing cash = ₹45,000.

Cash conversion cycle

CCC = Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days

A shorter cash conversion cycle generally means cash is tied up for less time. Monitor late customer payments, slow-moving stock and supplier payment terms.

Cash runway

Runway (months) = Available cash ÷ Average monthly net cash burn

Use a realistic forecast and update it frequently. A profitable business can still run out of cash if customers pay late or inventory grows too quickly.

Operations

9. Inventory and purchasing mathematics

Closing inventory = Opening inventory + Purchases − COGS (subject to adjustments)
Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Average inventory
Days inventory outstanding = 365 ÷ Inventory turnover
Reorder point = Average daily usage × Lead time + Safety stock
Economic order quantity (EOQ) = √(2DS ÷ H)

EOQ: D = annual demand in units; S = ordering cost per order; H = annual holding cost per unit. The model assumes stable demand and costs; use it as a guide, not an automatic order instruction.

Reorder example: Average sales 8 units/day, supplier lead time 6 days, safety stock 20 units. Reorder point = 8 × 6 + 20 = 68 units.

Track stockouts, shrinkage, expiry, damaged goods, returns and slow-moving inventory. Reconcile physical stock with records.

Marketing and sales

10. Sales, customer and marketing metrics

Revenue = Units sold × Average selling price
Average order value (AOV) = Revenue ÷ Number of orders
Conversion rate = Conversions ÷ Leads or visitors × 100
Customer acquisition cost (CAC) = Acquisition spend ÷ New customers acquired
Return on ad spend (ROAS) = Revenue attributed to ads ÷ Ad spend
Customer lifetime value (simple) = Average contribution per period × Expected customer periods
Example: Advertising spend ₹20,000 brings 40 new customers. CAC = ₹500 per customer. If attributed sales are ₹80,000, ROAS = 4×. ROAS is revenue-based, not profit; subtract product and fulfilment costs to assess profitability.

Measure repeat purchase rate, refund rate, churn, lead response time and contribution per customer—not only followers, views or gross revenue.

Decision-making

11. Contribution and break-even analysis

Contribution per unit = Selling price per unit − Variable cost per unit
Contribution margin ratio = Contribution ÷ Sales
Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution per unit
Break-even sales value = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin ratio
Target-profit units = (Fixed costs + Target operating profit) ÷ Contribution per unit
Example: Selling price ₹500; variable cost ₹300; monthly fixed costs ₹40,000. Contribution = ₹200/unit. Break-even = 40,000 ÷ 200 = 200 units. To earn ₹20,000 operating profit: (40,000 + 20,000) ÷ 200 = 300 units.
Break-even is a model. It assumes the selling price, variable cost and product mix stay sufficiently stable. Include realistic returns, discounts, taxes and capacity limits in planning.
Business accounts

12. Financial statements and key ratios

Income statement / profit and loss

Summarises revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, finance costs, tax and net profit over a period.

Balance sheet

Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity

Assets include cash, receivables, inventory and equipment. Liabilities include payables, loans and accrued expenses. Equity represents the owners' residual interest.

Cash flow statement

Groups cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit and cash flow are different because of credit sales, inventory, depreciation, loan principal and other timing differences.

RatioFormulaWhat it helps assess
Gross marginGross profit ÷ RevenueProduct economics
Net marginNet profit ÷ RevenueOverall profitability
Debt-to-equityTotal debt ÷ EquityLeverage
Receivables turnoverCredit sales ÷ Average receivablesCollection efficiency
Return on assetsNet income ÷ Average assetsAsset productivity
Return on equityNet income ÷ Average equityReturn to owners

Compare ratios over time and with similar businesses. Definitions can vary, so use consistent calculations.

Assets

13. Depreciation and asset replacement

Straight-line annual depreciation = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life
Written-down value = Cost − Accumulated depreciation
Example: Equipment costs ₹1,20,000, expected residual value ₹20,000 and useful life 5 years. Annual straight-line depreciation = (1,20,000 − 20,000) ÷ 5 = ₹20,000.

Depreciation allocates an asset's cost over its useful life; it is not the same as the cash spent to buy it. Accounting and tax depreciation rules may differ.

Planning with data

14. Statistics, forecasting and uncertainty

Mean = Sum of observations ÷ Number of observations
Median = Middle value after sorting the data
Range = Maximum − Minimum
Variance = Average squared deviation from the mean (population form)
Standard deviation = √Variance

Forecasting methods

  • Simple moving average: average the latest n periods.
  • Weighted moving average: assign higher weights to more relevant periods.
  • Growth forecast: Future value = Current value × (1 + growth rate)^periods.
  • Scenario analysis: build base, optimistic and pessimistic cases.
Example: Sales for three months are ₹90,000, ₹1,00,000 and ₹1,10,000. Three-month average = ₹1,00,000. This is a simple baseline, not a guarantee of next month's sales.

Probability and expected value

Expected value = Σ(Outcome × Probability of outcome)

Use probabilities for planning uncertain outcomes, but do not treat estimates as certainties. Test assumptions and keep a contingency reserve.

Expansion

15. Growth, investment and project evaluation

Compound growth = Present value × (1 + growth rate)^number of periods
Payback period = Initial investment ÷ Annual net cash inflow (when inflows are even)
ROI = (Net gain from investment ÷ Investment cost) × 100

Net present value (NPV)

NPV = Σ[Cash flow in period t ÷ (1 + discount rate)^t] − Initial investment

NPV compares future cash flows with today's money. A positive NPV indicates value creation under the chosen assumptions and discount rate. Use a consistent time period and include all relevant incremental cash flows.

Useful growth questions

  • Does the new product earn positive contribution after fulfilment and returns?
  • Can operations handle higher volume without quality falling?
  • How much cash will be tied up in stock and customer credit?
  • What happens if sales are 20% lower or costs 10% higher?
  • Does the return justify the risk and cost of capital?
Advanced tools

16. Algebra and quantitative decision tools

Linear equations

An equation balances two expressions. Use it to solve unknown price, quantity, cost or target sales.

If revenue = price × quantity, then quantity = revenue ÷ price.

Simultaneous equations

Useful when two unknowns are linked by two relationships, such as the sales mix of two products and total contribution.

Functions and marginal analysis

Profit = Revenue − Cost
Marginal revenue = Change in revenue ÷ Change in quantity
Marginal cost = Change in total cost ÷ Change in quantity

At a simplified profit-maximising output, marginal revenue equals marginal cost, subject to market, capacity and other constraints.

Logarithms and exponential growth

Useful for solving compound growth, doubling time and percentage growth over multiple periods.

Optimization and decision models

  • Linear programming: allocate limited labour, materials or machine hours to maximise contribution.
  • Decision trees: compare choices with uncertain outcomes and probabilities.
  • Sensitivity analysis: change one assumption at a time to see what drives the result.
  • Regression: estimate relationships, such as advertising spend and sales, while remembering correlation does not prove causation.

Practice questions

  1. A product costs ₹600 and sells for ₹750. Find profit and profit percentage on cost.
  2. Sales increase from ₹2,00,000 to ₹2,30,000. Find the growth percentage.
  3. A ₹2,500 item has a 12% discount. Find the discounted price before tax.
  4. Calculate 18% GST on a taxable value of ₹4,000 and the invoice total.
  5. Fixed costs are ₹60,000. Selling price is ₹800 and variable cost is ₹500. Find break-even units.
  6. Opening cash is ₹25,000, receipts are ₹90,000 and payments are ₹1,02,000. Find closing cash.
  7. Average daily demand is 12 units, lead time is 5 days and safety stock is 15 units. Find reorder point.
  8. A loan of ₹1,00,000 earns simple interest at 10% for 3 years. Find interest and total amount.
Show answers
  1. Profit ₹150; profit percentage = 150/600 × 100 = 25%.
  2. 15%.
  3. ₹2,200.
  4. GST ₹720; total ₹4,720.
  5. Contribution ₹300; break-even = 60,000/300 = 200 units.
  6. ₹13,000.
  7. 75 units.
  8. Interest ₹30,000; total ₹1,30,000.
